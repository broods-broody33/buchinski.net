Strategy · Field notes

The meeting isn't the decision

Most stalled plans didn't fail in the meeting. Everyone nodded, the slides looked good, the room agreed. Then nothing happened.

Agreement isn't the same as a decision. A decision has three parts: what we're doing, who owns it, and when we'll know it worked. Leave out any of them and you have a shared hope, not a plan.

The fix is a simple habit. Before anyone leaves, ask: "Who is doing what, by when, and how will we know?" Write the answer down where everyone can see it. If no one can answer, the decision isn't made yet, and that's fine. It's far better to find out in the room than three months later.

Clear ownership feels slower in the meeting. It's much faster everywhere else.

Operations · Field notes

Map the process people actually run

Every business has two versions of each process: the one in the manual and the one people actually follow. When something goes wrong, the second one is the one that matters.

Workarounds aren't failures. They're information. Each one marks a spot where the official process didn't fit the real work, and someone quietly fixed it. Spreadsheets on the side, a sticky note by the till, "just ask Sam": that's where the improvements are hiding.

So before redesigning anything, walk the real path end to end with the people doing it. Count the hand-offs. Note where work waits. Ask what they'd change if it were up to them. The answers are usually obvious, cheap and overdue.

Fix the real process and the manual can finally catch up.

Technology · Field notes

Buy the boring tool

Software demos are built to impress. Every feature looks essential when someone else is clicking through it. Then the subscription renews, the team uses a tenth of it, and the old spreadsheet is still open in another tab.

The better question isn't "what can this do?" It's "what will we still be using in three years?" That usually points to the boring choice: widely used, easy to learn, simple to get your data out of, and supported by someone who will still be around.

Start with the two or three jobs the tool really has to do well. Test those with the people who'll use it every day. Leave the rest of the feature list for later.

Technology should disappear into the work. If people talk about the tool more than the work, it's the wrong tool.
